Hackathon Challenges

Over one day, your team will tackle a real local question about urban heat and greenspace using Earth observation data. Each team chooses one of the three problem statements below and confirms its choice at the team meeting on Monday 30 November. The strongest outputs will answer a question someone in a council or public health team actually needs answered, and be honest about what the data can and can’t show.

All teams work in their EODH workspace with the datasets listed on the Data page. Every dataset is available to every team: each problem statement suggests a core set and other options, but you are free to mix them.

Note

Given the short timeframe, using datasets beyond those provided is generally discouraged. If you have a specific source in mind, check with the organisers at the 30 November or 2 December meeting.

Participation

Teams are pre-assigned and deliberately mixed. Each team includes at least one policy or public sector participant and two or three technical participants, supported by mentors from Imago and the EODH (see Mentors and judges). Policy participants frame the question and judge usefulness; technical participants make the data work. Both are essential.

Study area

Start with Newcastle upon Tyne. Teams who want a wider view can scale up to the North East Mayoral Strategic Authority area: Newcastle, Gateshead, North Tyneside, South Tyneside, Sunderland, County Durham and Northumberland. That lets you compare dense urban cores with suburbs, towns and rural areas. All datasets cover the full area.

MSOAs 34 253

Important

Choosing your scale

  • Few areas at MSOA level in Newcastle. With only 34 MSOAs, a correlation or model at MSOA level rests on 34 points, and splitting them into groups leaves very few in each. If you stay in Newcastle and want to estimate a relationship (challenges 2 and 3), work at LSOA level (180 areas), or scale up to the North East (253 MSOAs, 1,257 LSOAs).
  • Comparisons work well in Newcastle. Picking out the hottest and coolest few MSOAs from 34 (challenge 1) is manageable. Scaling up adds town and rural contrasts rather than statistical power.
  • Keep pixels local. The NCEO 100 m data are daily files across five summers. At North East scale that is a lot of data to process in a day without parallel computing. Use the Imago MSOA and LSOA products for the North East, and the pixels for Newcastle or a smaller pilot area.

1. What makes a hotspot?

Theme: Urban heat

Question: What sets the hottest neighbourhoods apart from the coolest in surface cover and built-up density? Start in Newcastle, then test whether the same features mark hotspots across the North East.

Core data

  • Imago Sentinel-3 heat indicators (MSOA), summer daytime, to pick out the hottest and coolest MSOAs.
  • Sentinel-2 summer composite (10 m), to describe them: vegetation (NDVI), built-up surfaces (NDBI) and water (NDWI).

Other options

  • Imago greenspace (LSOA) for share green.
  • HadUK-Grid air temperature (MSOA), to test whether hot surfaces also mean hot air.
  • Night-time Sentinel-3 indicators, to check whether daytime hotspots stay hot at night.
  • Population and deprivation table, to see who lives in the hotspots.

Go further

  • Choose a pilot area containing both hot and cool MSOAs and find hotspots within it at 100 m, using the Imago 100 m LSOA product or the NCEO 100 m pixels, instead of treating whole MSOAs as hot or cool.
  • At North East scale, compare hotspot features in city centres, towns and rural areas.

By 16:00: The hottest and coolest areas, a side-by-side comparison of their surface cover and built-up density, and three or four examples illustrated with Sentinel-2 imagery.

2. Does green cool?

Theme: Greenspace and cooling

Question: How strongly is vegetation linked to lower surface temperature, and where does that link break down? Does it hold beyond Newcastle, across the wider North East?

Core data

  • Imago Sentinel-3 heat indicators (MSOA), day and night.
  • NDVI from the Sentinel-2 summer composite, averaged for each area.
  • Imago greenspace (LSOA) as share green, rolled up to MSOA.

Other options

  • Imago Sentinel-3 heat indicators at LSOA.
  • HadUK-Grid air temperature (MSOA), to see whether greener areas also have cooler air.
  • Population and deprivation table, to describe the places where green doesn’t cool.

Go further

  • Estimate the LST–NDVI relationship at 100 m, using the NCEO 100 m pixels against NDVI resampled to 100 m, or the Imago 100 m LSOA product, and check whether it holds at both scales and both times of day.
  • At North East scale, test whether the relationship differs between urban and rural areas.

By 16:00: A plot of the LST–NDVI relationship for day and night, and a map of places that are hotter than their greenness predicts, with a short note on why for two or three of them.

3. Who lives in the heat?

Theme: Unequal exposure

Question: Is exposure to daytime and night-time heat unequal across social groups, and how much of the gap is linked to differences in greenspace? Compare Newcastle with the North East as a whole.

Core data

  • Imago Sentinel-3 heat indicators (MSOA), day and night.
  • Imago greenspace (LSOA) and NDVI from Sentinel-2.
  • The population and deprivation table (LSOA), aggregated to MSOA and weighted by population.

Other options

  • HadUK-Grid air temperature (MSOA), including heat extremes, as a measure closer to what people feel. It also has cold extremes, so you can check whether the same groups are exposed to both heat and cold.
  • Imago Sentinel-3 heat indicators at LSOA.

Go further

  • Work at LSOA level with the Imago 100 m LSOA product, joined directly to greenspace and the LSOA table.
  • Use the NCEO 100 m pixels to look at day-to-day variation during hot spells in specific neighbourhoods.

By 16:00:

  • Heat exposure gaps for two or three groups (for example most vs least deprived areas, older residents, social renters).
  • A simple model showing how each gap changes once greenspace is accounted for.
  • A one-slide policy summary for Insights North East. It should note that these are area-level patterns, not measures of what individuals experience.
